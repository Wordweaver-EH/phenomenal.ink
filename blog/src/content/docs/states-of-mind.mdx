> **The mind is a kind of theatre, where several perceptions successively make their appearance; pass, repass, glide away, and mingle in an infinite variety of postures and situations**. - David Hume, Treatise on Human Nature

Read these words now, now notice they turn into thoughts. Each word evokes an idea. Each idea is replaced by the next. Notice how they relate to combine, notice how some stay at hand - some back of the mind, and some into oblivion at the tip of the tongue. Begin from left to right, top to bottom the words. So does experience move through time. What is left, is left to memory, what will be is right ahead but what is, will stay in this instant. David Hume thought this to be a [train of thought](https://iep.utm.edu/associationism-in-philosophy-of-mind/#:~:text=According%20to%20Hume%2C%20the%20trains,these%20simple%20ideas%20by%20association) or a chain of reason. William James disagreed and thought consciousness to be an unjointed stream, flowing continuously, ever changing and never quite the same. But chain or stream, there is something that it is *like* to be you right now. It feels like *something*  and that something can be hard to put into words sometimes. There is a poverty of language when it comes to feeling that we run into when it comes to experiences profoundly altered from our everyday way of being or even everyday experiences that we have never had to put words to before. But we needn’t be feel alone, for we are in good company, because at the dawn of science, Humphry Davy inhaled 20 quarts of laughing gas.

Davy was a poet, a romantic, and a scientist beyond being delirious on nitrous oxide in the year 1799. Language was important to him, in that time when there was little distinction between art, science, philosophy — his experiments were demonstrations in spectacle and wonder. I was struck by the words his participants would use to describe their experience [^1]. They lacked the language then, and they too would use wonder to describe this alteration in experience.

Can we do any better?

[The Age of Wonder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Age_of_Wonder) lies dead, [disenchantment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disenchantment) as Max Weber would say has won. # Altered States

General Anesthesia is the science and art of turning subjects into objects. A person becomes a thing as the continuity of self is disrupted. You do not think, therefore, you are not, Hume might say [^4]. It is a miracle of modern science and if you asked the modern anesthesiologist about mapping consciousness, he would likely show you this graph:  

![Circumplex_model_of_emotion.svg](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/ad/Circumplex_model_of_emotion.svg/900px-Circumplex_model_of_emotion.svg.png?20230607155726)

This maps alterations in consciousness along two dimensions, arousal and awareness. Seeing the drift of this, an astute reader might ask:

> *“But Weaver, you aren’t an anesthesiologist, you claim you are a hypnotist?”*

This is true.

> *”Where is hypnosis then, the altered state of consciousness? Where is trance?”*

Ah. Therein I diverge from much of the consensus [^5]. I don’t think it is a thing. Or to put the emphasis on the right syllable:

I don’t think it is ***A*** thing. 

In hypnosis, trance is a result of suggestion (usually, the induction), which is not to say it is not a real alteration in state but to say it is not a “special state”. Or even necessarily, one that can be called an altered state of consciousness in our anesthesiologist’s book (unless perhaps, we are talking about inductions of sleep). The alteration lies in the broader construct of the mental states but it is meaningless, in my opinion, to claim altered states if we cannot explain it  what way it is altered. Arousal and awareness are but two dimensions that our instructions and suggestions might alter state in, sleep inductions work after all (and so do bedtime stories). And often the novice hypnotist mistakes going down the diagonal of the above chart as the direction of “depth” and the more relaxed and less aware the subject is, the more suggestible they might be expected to. This is foolish.  Response to suggestions is a skill and skills are trait-like. They are stable through periods of time, an often cited study shows performance on a standard hypnosis scale to be stable through 25 years for a cohort [^6]. But performance of skills is also state-like, it varies from time to time based on state of mind and furthermore, different skills have different optimal states, the relaxed focus of olympic archery is different from the aggression of the powerlifter at the meet, they vary in the dimension of arousal. An example of this can be seen in the Phenomenological Control Scale study from Zoltan Dienes’ group where they give the same set of suggestions to two groups, one gets a relaxation induction where the word hypnosis is used in preparation and the other a staircase induction where the label of hypnosis is not used. The hypnosis group performs markedly worse in the motor items than the group without hypnosis, and it could be as the authors say that the students were negatively polarized against hypnosis but I think I have seen it enough times in my practice that a person who is relaxed, simply has a harder time moving voluntarily or involuntarily. So, no, I am not being completely coy here, I do think the alterations in state we induce are real and they matter to subsequent response and if we so choose, we can directly test this hypothesis in the future. 

Another dimension of state, that taken with arousal is often used in suggestions is valence, that you aren’t just relaxing but you are also feeling good. Taken together, they give us the circumplex model of mood:

There isn’t much science here yet, there is a hypothesis from [Kev Sheldrake’s Head Hacking days](http://rtfc.org.uk/FreeYourMindPart3.html) that heightened emotions might help response. I am sympathetic to the view but I think the kind of emotion and suggestion have to be congruent, having someone very afraid might make them more likely  to see ghosts in the dark but unless they are a particular kind of person, it will not lead to a [spontaneous orgasm](https://www.cosmopolitan.com/uk/love-sex/sex/a25421757/erotic-hypnosis/). Awareness, our second dimension from the anesthesiologist’s chart, then matters to how response occurs because ultimately, we must wrangle how Hume’s train of thought is put on track. Awareness has a breadth to it, how many ideas do we hold in our attention? We zone out sometimes and think of nothing and others we think of but a few, overcome with emotion, others we are distracted, letting our minds wander, hither and tither between many thoughts. This number of thoughts we can have is constrained by processes outside of conscious control. Then, there is a depth in time to it, how long do we hold one of them in our awareness? Attention then, this deliberate process, correlates with how long we can keep task relevant thought in our mind while suppressing, constraining other thoughts while automatic processes both invoke new thoughts and constrain their number. The dynamic framework of thought makes a pretty picture between these automatic and deliberate constraints.
