> *The mind is a kind of theatre, where several perceptions successively make their appearance; pass, repass, glide away, and mingle in an infinite variety of postures and situations*.
><br/>-- *David Hume, Treatise on Human Nature (1739–40)*

Read these words now. Notice how they turn into thoughts. Each word evokes an idea. Each idea is replaced by the next. Notice how they relate to combine, notice how some stay at hand - some back of the mind, and some into a vague memory at the tip of the tongue. And as the words go from left to right, top to bottom so does experience move through time. What is left, is left to memory, what will be is right ahead but what is, will stay in this instant. David Hume thought this to be a [train of thought](https://iep.utm.edu/associationism-in-philosophy-of-mind/#:~:text=According%20to%20Hume%2C%20the%20trains,these%20simple%20ideas%20by%20association) or a chain of reason. William James disagreed and thought consciousness to be an unjointed stream, flowing continuously, ever-changing, and never quite the same. But chain or stream, there is something that it is *like* to be you right now. It feels like *something*  and that something can be hard to put into words sometimes. There is a poverty of language when it comes to feeling that we run into when it comes to experiences profoundly altered from our everyday way of being or even everyday experiences that we have never had to put words to before. But we needn’t feel alone, for we are in good company because at the dawn of science, Humphry Davy inhaled 20 quarts of laughing gas.  

![Davy-Cartoon.png](https://the-public-domain-review.imgix.net/essays/o-excellent-air-bag-humphry-davy-and-nitrous-oxide/14650968627_5c7e22c444_c.jpg?w=800)

> “Nothing exists but thoughts!—the universe is composed of impressions, ideas, pleasures and pains!”  
> A cartoon featured in an 1807 dissertation by a medical student at the University of Pennsylvania on the “chemical and exhilarating effects of nitrous oxide gas.” Around the two figures, presumably Davy to the right and perhaps Beddoes to the left, a number of Davy’s quotes describing his experiences appear — Source, The Wellcome Library.

Davy was a poet, a romantic, and a scientist beyond being delirious on nitrous oxide in the year 1799. Language was important to him, in that time when there was little distinction between art, science, and philosophy — his experiments were demonstrations in spectacle and wonder. He was struck by the words his participants would use to describe their experience [^1]. They lacked the language then, and  theory too to describe this alteration in experience.

[^1]: One report goes, “I feel like a harp sounds!” many others can be found [here](https://publicdomainreview.org/collection/the-nitrous-oxide-experiments-of-humphry-davy/), including one from an amateur chemist, Samuel Taylor Coleridge who was known to have a way with [words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Rime_of_the_Ancient_Mariner) and coined the phrase “suspension of disbelief.”

This essay is somewhat of a commemoration. It has been 225 years (to the day the first excerpt of this essay was posted) since Davy’s Boxing Day Experiment with the first surgical anesthetic [^2]. 240 years since the Franklin Commission indicted Anton Mesmer’s animal magnetism to be a result of compression, imagination, and suggestion. 240 years also since Mesmer’s acolyte, Marquis de Puységur stumbled his way into the “magnetic sleep,” which perhaps was a link in the chain to the psychological turn of the Enlightenment [^3]. [The Age of Wonder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Age_of_Wonder) lies dead, [disenchantment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disenchantment), perhaps has already won. [Trance should be dead](https://www.cosmic-pancakes.com/blog/barber-scientific?MerryXmasKev&Amy), but can we re-enchant the disenchanted? Because wonder is the first step to understanding, and beyond it lies Awe.
