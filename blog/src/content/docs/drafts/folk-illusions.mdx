> "Seeing—in this case—is not believing; visual perception—like rubber—is malleable. The Rubber Pencil illusion is a lesson as much as it is play.
>
> As a cultural tradition passed along via word of mouth from child to child for generation after generation, the Rubber Pencil illusion is also folklore.  [...]  The outsider to the trick turns insider before the look of astonishment disappears from her face. Spreading the word, knowledge begets play begets knowledge begets play.
>
> -- Folk Illusions, Claiborne Rice, K. Brandon Barker

It was a sunny day, that much I remember. Monsoon, that Indian season of rains, had ended and we could go out into the school yard for recess and play. I was scheming some schoolboy scheme with a friend when a boy from the third grade, a year above us approached us. 

*"Have you learned about magnets yet?*" he asked gravely. 

We replied that we had indeed learned about magnets in the science class, affronted that it should even be a question that us two *second graders* could be ignorant about magnets of all things. 

*"Did you learn about how if you stroke a magnet along a piece of iron, the iron too becomes magnetic?"* he continued. 

Yes, we chorused, indignation rising. Why, just a few weeks ago, on a saturday morning, I had painstakingly stroked an iron needle with the north pole of a magnet a hundred and one times and set it afloat carefully on the meniscus of a bowl of water to see it point North. 

*"Did you know that if you took any two pebbles from the yard here and rubbed them three times clockwise and five times counterclockwise [^1], that the stones will become magnetic and begin to attract each other?"*
[^1]: Terms we had also recently learned.

This finally we had to confess that we did not know but we wouldn't have been budding scientists to believe that without empirically testing it ourselves [^2]. I picked up the two rocks and rubbed them clockwise and counterclockwise, forgetting which one is which and starting all over again but I managed it finally and held them millimeters apart trying to feel the force between them and it was there! It was real and the rocks came together in my hand. It was very strange and I had a vague forboding that I was on the precipice of hidden, forbidden physics that the adults either did not know about or had decided to keep from us. I was seven then and fourteen years later, as my interest in hypnosis and suggestion became a practical pursuit that I had started to learn the ropes of, I came across a classic exercise in suggestion called "Magnetic Hands". It is often called a "convincer" by stage and street hypnotists and has been a mainstay in the various scales aimed at measuring hypnosis since 1960s. The similarity of this exercise to that early, nearly forgotten memory of the rocks magnetized by ritual, I am ashamed to say, was completely lost on me. That is until a chance encounter with this charming book during a wild goose chase of a google search a few years ago. And it has been one of my favourite books on hypnosis that aren't actually about hypnosis ever since [^3].

> D2. Magnetic Rocks  Magic Rocks and its variants constitute another set of traditionalized Kohnstamm illusions. The actor is told to place a rock in each hand. He is then instructed to press the rocks together very firmly. An incantation is performed by the director; this incantation allows for an allotted amount of time to pass. When the rocks are slowly pulled apart, the actor feels as though they are magnetic. 
>
> Remembrance gathered from a folklore student, spring 2011, played as a child, Memphis, Tennessee; remembrance gathered from a folklore student, fall 2013, played with brothers around eight years old, Fairfield, Connecticut. 

[^2]: Nullius in verba, that motto of Enlightenment hadn't explicitly been taught to us in second grade but second graders scarcely take anyone's word, I think. The rebellious spirit of curiousity that typified the figurative children of Enlightenment is not too far, I think, from that of actual children figuring the world out while it is still new to them.

[^3]: These kinds of books that are nominally not about hypnosis but to the enthusiast seem inexorably secretly about hypnosis, I am convinced are the best books about hypnosis. This notion of mine perhaps is why I never really seem to want to talk about hypnosis directly here.
