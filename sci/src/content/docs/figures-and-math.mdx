Matrix looks nice too!

$$

**Numbered equations example**

You can also use `\begin{equation} ... \end{equation}` in case you prefer numbered equations:

$$
\begin{equation} \begin{pmatrix}
a & b \cr
c & d
\end{pmatrix}
+
\begin{pmatrix}
e & f \cr
g & h
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}
$$

$$
\begin{equation} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 14 \\ 32 \\ 50 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation}
$$

$$

# The Physics of Phosphenes

We often think of vision as a direct window to the outside world, but sometimes the light comes from within. Phosphenes—the experience of seeing light without light entering the eye—reveal the mechanical and electrical nature of our visual system [^1].

## Mechanical Phosphenes

The most common way to experience a phosphene is by applying gentle pressure to the side of your closed eye. This mechanical deformation of the retina stimulates the photoreceptors, which the brain interprets as light. Since the retina maps the visual field in an inverted manner, pressure on the left side of the eye produces a visual sensation on the right.

### Electrical Stimulation

Phosphenes can also be induced electrically. Brief magnetic pulses delivered to the visual cortex (TMS) can create flashes of light, mapping out the functional layout of our visual processing centers.

![Isaac Newton experimenting with phosphenes](/images/newton-phosphene.png)

<Sidenote>
  Isaac Newton famously (and recklessly) experimented with phosphenes by inserting a bodkin behind his eye to deform the retina.
</Sidenote>

[^1]: This phenomenon is distinct from hallucinations caused by pharmacological agents, though the underlying neural mechanisms may overlap.
